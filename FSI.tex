ABSTRACT:
A new Eulerian method is presented for simulating complex three dimensional
and three dimensional axisymmetric flows
consisting of any number of materials, to include compressible
fluids, incompressible fluids, ice, melt, vapor, and 
solids undergoing plastic deformation.  The interfaces between materials
are modeled and discretized as sharp.  The new method is robust with respect 
to a wide range of material properties, and large interface deformation.  
The new method is scalable on MIMD (Multi Instruction Distributed memory using
MPI) and MISD (Multi Instructuion Shared memory using OpenMP) computer systems.
The new method is also scalable with respect to 
dynamic adaptive mesh refinement.

\section{Introduction}

The ability to simulate multiphase and multimaterial high and low 
speed flows,
to include the interaction of fluid(s) and elastic material(s),
has a number of applications:
bubble growth and collapse interacting with flexible
surfaces\cite{xiong2026bubble,xu2019experimental,wang2017multiphase},
wetting dynamics of liquid droplets on deformable 
membranes\cite{MokbelMokbelLieseWeberAland2024},
design of breathable masks\cite{dressaire2015drop}, 
underwater explosions near 
structures\cite{leger2023experimental,yin2022experimental,chen2026numerical,
gao2025study,he2024research,wardlaw2000fluid,sandusky1999dynamic}, 
hydroelastic applications\cite{MENG2022114522},
vibration of pipes due to internal boiling flow\cite{AN2016201},
ultrasonic leak detection in nuclear power plants\cite{choi2024novel},
and the measurement of the tensile strength properties of colliding
and deforming structures\cite{cui2024oblique}.

We are not aware of previously reported research on the robust numerical
simulation of the interaction of multiphase flows with elastic materials, 
to include incompressible materials, weakly compressible materials, and/or
strongly compressible materials.  The research that we report here is a
continuation of research that started with the following
article by Jemison et al\cite{jemison2014compressible}.  In Jemison et al's
research, a novel adaptive, parallel, asymptotically 
preserving\cite{kwatra2009method,BIBAL2024112750,boscarino2018all,
scandurra2016numerical},
moment-of-fluid method was presented
for simulating two phase flows to include incompressible, low speed, and
high speed flows.  The method in Jemison et al was a collocated generalization
of the projection method for incompressible flows\cite{jemison2013coupled} 
in order to simulate both compressible (with shock waves) and 
incompressible flows.

In the present work, we have improved upon 
\cite{jemison2014compressible}
in the following important ways:
\begin{description}
\item[1.] the present method is now a staggered grid
  method instead of colocated.  
  See Figures 
  \ref{EILE},
  \ref{prolongation},
  and \ref{recombination}.  
  The
  following research for incompressible flows underscore the
  importance of a staggered grid\cite{LECHENADEC2013185,
  sugiyama2011full,shin2020interaction,GUITTET2015215} for
  projection methods.  We also report recent work by
  Park and Son\cite{PARK2026132} in which they developed
  a staggered grid method for compressible flows in which the
  acoustic time step constraint was avoided.
\item[2.] the present method represents complex deforming interfaces
  using the Continuous Moment-of-Fluid method\cite{ye2024improved} (CMOF),
  instead of the Moment-of-Fluid (MOF) method\cite{jemison2015filament}.
  The CMOF method\cite{ye2024improved} removes a ``checkerboard instability''
  that appears for surface tension driven flows.
\item[3.] We have developed a conservative, staggered grid, algorithm for
  solving the equations associated with standard elastic materials and
  materials undergoing plastic deformation. Our new algorithm is an 
  improvement on the algorithms reported in
  \cite{sugiyama2011full,shin2020interaction,PARK2026132} so that we can
  simulate both low and high speed fluid structure interaction problems.
  Our new method can simulate incompressible elastic materials or 
  compressible elastic materials with shock waves.
\item[4.] We have developed an exactly mass and volume preserving algorithm
	so that mass and volume of fluid and elastic materials do not
	change due to contact line dynamics (e.g. splashing or sliding), or 
	flotsam removal.
\end{description}

\subsection{Motivation for enhancing our asymptotically preserving
  algorithm from collocated grid to staggered grid }

In this section, we just focus on single material inviscid
compressible gas dynamics
with a general equation of state and periodic boundary conditions.  
The derivations here are applicable to the
general multimaterial case.  
The compressible gas dynamic 
equations\cite{almgren2006low,boscarino2018all,scandurra2016numerical,
CHERTOCK2026119292} are:
\begin{eqnarray}
\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u})=0 \label{pde1}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho \vec{u})_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}+p\EyeTen)=0
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho E)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}E+p\vec{u})=0
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
E=e+\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}\cdot\vec{u}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
p=p(\rho,e) \hspace{20pt} c^{2}=p_{\rho}+\frac{p_{e}p}{\rho^{2}} 
\hspace{20pt} e=c_{v}T \label{pde2}
\end{eqnarray}

Following the ``asymptotically preserving'' analysis in these articles,
\cite{almgren2006low,boscarino2018all,scandurra2016numerical,
CHERTOCK2026119292}, 
it can be shown that our
Directionally split Cell Integrated Semi-Lagrangian (CISL) algorithm 
given by
Algorithm \ref{asym_preserve}
approaches a discretization consistent with the incompressible flow
equations in the zero Mach number limit.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Asymptotically Preserving time discretization for gas dynamics\label{asym_preserve}}
\begin{algorithmic}[1] % [1] enables line numbering
\State $k \gets 0$
\State $\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)} \gets \vec{u}^{n}$
\While{$k \le 1$}
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets \rho^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$ 
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}\vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets (\rho\vec{u})^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$

 \State $p^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets p(\rho^{n+1,(k+1)},e^{\ast,(k+1)})$
 \State $c^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets c(\rho^{n+1,(k+1)},e^{\ast,(k+1)})$
 \State $\left( \begin{array}{c} \vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)} \\ 
           p^{n+1,(k+1)} \end{array}\right)\gets
  \left( \begin{array}{c} 
    \vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t\frac{\nabla p^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}} \\

 \State $E^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets E^{\ast,(k+1)}-
	\Delta t\frac{\nabla\cdot(\vec{u}p)^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}}$

\EndWhile

\subsection{Lax diffusion for Energy and Momentum equations in 
elastic materials }

In this section, we just focus on single material compressible 
elastic fluid dynamics
with a general equation of state and periodic boundary conditions.  
The derivations here are applicable to the
general multimaterial case.  
\par\noindent
The compressible elastic fluid dynamic 
equations\cite{tran2004particle,maire2013nominally,camacho1997adaptive} are:
\begin{eqnarray}
\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u})=0 \label{pde1elastic}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho \vec{u})_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}+p\EyeTen-s)=0
\label{elastic_mom}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho E)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}E+p\vec{u}-\vec{u}\cdot s)=0
\label{energy_elastic}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
E=e+\frac{1}{2}\vec{u}\cdot\vec{u}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho s)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}s)= 
\rho(\Omega s-s\Omega+2G(\bar{D}-D^{p}))
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
D=\frac{\nabla\vec{u}+(\nabla\vec{u})^{T}}{2}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
\Omega_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(
\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-
\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}})
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
p=p(\rho,e) \hspace{20pt} c^{2}=p_{\rho}+\frac{p_{e}p}{\rho^{2}} 
\hspace{20pt} e=c_{v}T \label{pde2elastic}
\end{eqnarray}
$D^{p}$ is the plastic strain rate and is defined 
below (\ref{plastic_strain_rate}).
\par\noindent
The elastic tensor $s$ is discretized on a staggered grid as 
illustrated in Figures 
\ref{EILE},
\ref{prolongation},
and \ref{recombination},
and as recommended by
\cite{sugiyama2011full,shin2020interaction,PARK2026132}.
\par\noindent
We discretize the elastic term, 
$\nabla\cdot s$ in (\ref{elastic_mom}), and
the $\nabla\cdot(\vec{u}p)$ term in (\ref{energy_elastic}) using 
central differencing for which we have found to cause problems in elastic
materials for realistic values of the shear modulus $G$.  
In order to overcome 
this difficulty, we add Lax Friedrichs Diffusion\cite{Lax1954} 
to both the momentum equation (\ref{elastic_mom}),
\begin{eqnarray}
\nabla\cdot (2\mu_{\mbox{Lax}}D),
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
\mu_{\mbox{Lax}}=\frac{\rho_{\mbox{max}}\Delta x^{2}}{2\mbox{DIM}\Delta t}
\hspace{20pt} D=\frac{\nabla\vec{u}+(\nabla\vec{u})^{T}}{2} \hspace{20pt}
\mbox{DIM}=2 \mbox{ or } 3,
\end{eqnarray}
and the energy equation (\ref{energy_elastic}),
\begin{eqnarray}
\nabla\cdot (k_{\mbox{Lax}}\nabla T),
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
k_{\mbox{Lax}}=\frac{\rho_{\mbox{max}}c_{v}\Delta x^{2}}{2\mbox{DIM}\Delta t}.
\end{eqnarray}
So, an outline of our asymptotically preserving
temporal discretization of non-isothermal gas dynamics,
including Lax-Friedrichs smoothing, is given by Algorithm \ref{asym_preserve_elastic}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Asymptotically Preserving time discretization for an elastic material\label{asym_preserve_elastic}}
\begin{algorithmic}[1] % [1] enables line numbering
\State $k \gets 0$
\State $\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)} \gets \vec{u}^{n}$
\While{$k \le 1$}
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets \rho^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$ 
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}\vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets (\rho\vec{u})^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}E^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets (\rho E)^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho E \vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$
 \State $\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}s^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets (\rho s)^{n}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\nabla\cdot(\rho s \vec{u}^{n+1,(k)})dt$
 \State $s^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets s^{\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t(\Omega^{\ast,(k+1)}s^{\ast,(k+1)}-s^{\ast,(k+1)}\Omega^{\ast,(k+1)}+2G(\bar{D}^{\ast,(k+1)}-(D^{p})^{\ast,(k+1)}))$
 \State $\vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets\vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t \frac{\nabla\cdot (2(\mu+\mu_{\mbox{Lax}})D^{\ast\ast,(k+1)})}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}}$
 \State $T^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets T^{\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t \frac{\nabla\cdot ((k+k_{\mbox{Lax}})\nabla T^{\ast\ast,(k+1)})}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}c_{v}}$
 \State $\vec{u}^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets\vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t\frac{\nabla\cdot s^{\ast,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}}$
 \State $T^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets T^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t\frac{\nabla\vec{u}^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)}:s^{\ast,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}c_{v}}$
 \State $p^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets p(\rho^{n+1,(k+1)},e^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)})$
 \State $c^{\ast,(k+1)}\gets c(\rho^{n+1,(k+1)},e^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)})$
 \State $\left( \begin{array}{c} \vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)} \\ 
           p^{n+1,(k+1)} \end{array}\right)\gets
  \left( \begin{array}{c} 
    \vec{u}^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t\frac{\nabla p^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}} \\
  p^{\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t \rho^{n+1,(k+1)} (c^{\ast,(k+1)})^{2} \nabla\cdot\vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)}\end{array}\right)$
\State $E^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets E^{\ast\ast\ast,(k+1)}-
	\Delta t\frac{\nabla\cdot(\vec{u}p)^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\rho^{n+1,(k+1)}}$
 \State $k \gets k+1$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Remarks:
\begin{description}
\item[1.] In \cite{Lax1954}, the artificial diffusion term(s) are discretized
  explicitly in time, but in our implementation, diffusion is discretized
  in time semi-implicitly; see steps 9 and 10 in 
  Algorithm \ref{asym_preserve_elastic}.
\item[2.] In \cite{Lax1954}, it is proved for general scalar hyperbolic
  problems that the Lax method converges if the CFL condition
  is met.  For systems of hyperbolic conservation laws, Lax 
  conjectures that the method converges, but does not offer a proof.  
  We have a similar conjecture, that Lax artificial diffusion guarantees 
  convergence; our conjecture is stronger.  We conjecture
  that adding Lax artificial diffusion to our method guarantees
  convergence so long as the ``velocity'' CFL condition and 
  ``shear modulus'' CFL conditions are met (\ref{CFLcondition}), 
  but sound speed restrictions
  on the time step are unnecessary.
\item[3.] We have done extensive testing of our numerical method and have
  found that Lax artificial diffusion to be necessary only for stiff
  elastic materials.  We have found that adding Lax diffusion to 
  liquid materials would impose excessive damping.
\item[4.] We are only aware of proofs for the Godunov method and
  fractional step Lax-Friedrichs method for isentropic gas
  dynamics\cite{xiaxi1989convergence}.  Neither of these methods
  are asymptotically preserving.
\item[5.] Our numerical algorithm never requires the solution of 
  nonlinear algebraic equations, thereby avoiding robustness problems
  in which a discrete solution cannot be found or avoiding problems in
  which the discrete solution is not unique.
\end{description}

\section{Equations for Multiphase and Multimaterial flows interacting
 with elastic materials.}

We assume that we have $M$ materials total consisting of $M_{e}$ elastic 
materials and $M_{f}$ fluid materials.
Referring to Figure
\ref{materials}, the elastic materials occupy the regions in which
the elastic level set function\cite{osher1988fronts}, 
$\phi_{m_{e}}$, is positive:
\begin{eqnarray}
 \Omega_{m_{e}}(t)=\left\{ (\vec{x},t) | \phi_{m_{e}}(\vec{x},t)\ge 0 \right\}
\end{eqnarray}
A fluid material, $m_{f}$, occupies the region in which all of the elastic
level set functions\cite{osher1988fronts} 
are negative, and the fluid material level set
function is positive:
\begin{eqnarray}
\Omega_{m_{f}}(t)=\left\{ (\vec{x},t) | \phi_{m_{e}}(\vec{x},t)< 0 \mbox{ and }
  \phi_{m_{f}}(\vec{x},t)> 0 \right\}
\end{eqnarray}

Also, referring to Figure \ref{materials}, we discretize the interface(s)
using the ``continuous moment of fluid method''\cite{ye2024improved}.
The volume fraction and centroid in a given computational cell, $\Omega$,
are defined as:
\begin{eqnarray}
F_{m}=\frac{\int_{\Omega} H(\phi_{m})d\vec{x}}{|\Omega|}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
\vec{x}_{m}=\frac{\int_{\Omega} \vec{x}H(\phi_{m})d\vec{x}}{|\Omega|F_{m}}
\end{eqnarray}
$H(\phi)$ is the Heaviside function defined as:
\begin{eqnarray}
	H(\phi)=\left\{ \begin{array}{cc}
		1 & \phi\ge 0 \\
		0 & \phi<0 
	\end{array} \right.
\end{eqnarray}

\subsection{Governing Equations in the bulk regions of a given material }

In the bulk regions of a given material we have the following
equations for compressible materials\cite{jemison2014compressible}:
\begin{eqnarray}
	\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u})=0
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	(\rho\vec{u})_{t}+\nabla\cdot(
	\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}+p\EyeTen-\tau)=\vec{F}_{body}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	(\rho E)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(
	(\rho E + p)\vec{u}-\vec{u}\cdot\tau-k\nabla T)=
	\vec{u}\cdot \vec{F}_{body}
	\label{energy}
\end{eqnarray}

\begin{comment}
\begin{eqnarray}
	(\rho Y)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho Y\vec{u})=\nabla\cdot(\rho D\nabla Y)
\end{eqnarray}
\end{comment}

\begin{eqnarray}
	\tau=2\mu\bar{D}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	\bar{D}=D-\frac{\mbox{Trace}(D)\EyeTen}{\mbox{Trace}(\EyeTen)}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	D=\frac{\nabla\vec{u}+(\nabla\vec{u})^{T}}{2}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	\rho E=\rho e_{\mbox{int}}+
	\frac{1}{2}\rho|\vec{u}|^{2}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
p=p(\rho,e) \hspace{20pt} e=c_{v}T \hspace{20pt}
c^{2}=p_{\rho}+\frac{p_{e}p}{\rho^{2}}
\end{eqnarray}
If the material is a viscoelastic FENE-CR material, then one has the
additional equation for the configuration tensor 
$A$\cite{stewart2008improved},
%StewartLaySussmanOhta
\begin{eqnarray}
	(\rho A)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u} A)=
       \rho( A\cdot(\nabla\vec{u})^{T}+
	\nabla\vec{u}\cdot A-\frac{f(R)}{\lambda}(A-\EyeTen)) ,
\end{eqnarray}
and the momentum constitutive law is modified,
%units of div tau should be (kg/m^3)(m/s^2)
%units of tau should (kg/m^3)(m^2/s^2)=(kg/(s^2 m))=1 Pa
\begin{eqnarray}
	\tau=\tau+\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{P}}{\lambda}f(R)A.
\label{nonnewtonian}
\end{eqnarray}
If the material is an elastic material undergoing plastic deformation, then
one has the following additional equation for the deviatoric stress
tensor\cite{tran2004particle,UDAYKUMAR2003136,maire2013nominally}
% units of D are (1/s) units of s are the same as G.  Units of G are
% Pascal=N/m^2=kg (m/s^2)/m^2=kg/(s^2 m)
% G/\rho=kg/(s^2 m) (m^3/kg)=m^2/s^2
\begin{eqnarray}
(\rho s)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}s)= 
\rho(\Omega s-s\Omega+2G(\bar{D}-D^{p})), \label{plastic}
\end{eqnarray}
and the momentum constitutive law is modified,
\begin{eqnarray}
\tau=\tau+s.
\end{eqnarray}
If the material is an elastic material undergoing plastic deformation, then
the plastic strain equation is solved too,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\epsilon^{p}_{t}+\vec{u}\cdot\nabla\epsilon^{p}=
	\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}|\Lambda|.
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	|\Lambda|=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{D^{p}:D^{p}}
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	D^{p}=\Lambda N  \hspace{10pt} N=\sqrt{3/2}\frac{s}{S_{e}}
	\hspace{10pt} (\dot{\epsilon}^{p})^{2}=
	\frac{2}{3}\Lambda^{2}
	\label{plastic_strain_rate}
\end{eqnarray}
$\Omega$ is the spin tensor,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\Omega_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(
	\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-
	\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}})
\end{eqnarray}
The effective stress is defined as,
\begin{eqnarray}
	S_{e}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{s:s}
\end{eqnarray}
The Johnson-Cook model yield stress is given by,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\sigma_{y}=
	(A+B(\epsilon^{p})^{n})
	(1+C\ln(
	\frac{\dot{\epsilon}^{p}}
	{\dot{\epsilon}_{0}}
	))(1-\theta^{m}) \hspace{10pt}
	\theta=\frac{T-T_{0}}{T_{m}-T_{0}}
	\label{Johnson}
\end{eqnarray}
One form of the Zerilli Armstrong model yield stress is given by,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\beta=\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}
	\log(\dot{\epsilon}^{p}/\dot{\epsilon}^{p}_{ref})
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	\sigma_{y}=A+B(\epsilon^{p})^{n}e^{-\beta T}
	\label{zerilli}
\end{eqnarray}

The energy contribution due to elasto-plastic deformation is modeled as,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\beta\dot{\epsilon}^{p}S_{e},
\end{eqnarray}
and is added to (\ref{energy}).  In order to determine $\Lambda$, as it
appears in (\ref{plastic_strain_rate}), a ``radial
return algorithm'' is implemented as given by 
Algorithm \ref{radial_return_algorithm}.  We refer the reader also to 
\cite{tran2004particle} for the case when $n=1$ in (\ref{Johnson})
or (\ref{zerilli}).

%\If \ElseIf \Else \EndIf
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Radial return algorithm for determining the
plastic strain rate tensor $D^{p}$.\label{radial_return_algorithm}}

\begin{algorithmic}[1] % [1] enables line numbering
\State $k \gets 0$
\State $\dot{\epsilon}^{p}\gets 0$
\State $S_{e} \gets \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{s:s}$
\While{$k \le 1$}
\State $\sigma_{y} \gets \sigma_{y}(T,\epsilon^{p},\dot{\epsilon}^{p})$
\If {$S_{e}\le\sigma_{y}$} 
\State $\Lambda \gets 0$, $D^{p}\gets 0$
\Else
\State Solve for $\epsilon^{p}_{new}$: $
		S_{e}-\sigma_{y}=3G(\epsilon^{p}_{new}-\epsilon^{p})+
		B((\epsilon^{p}_{new})^{n}-
		  (\epsilon^{p})^{n}) $
\State $\dot{\epsilon}^{p} \gets \frac{
			 \epsilon^{p}_{new}-\epsilon^{p}}{\Delta t} $
\State $\Lambda \gets \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\dot{\epsilon}^{p}$
\State $D^{p} \gets \Lambda N$
\EndIf
\State $k \gets k+1$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Governing Equations at immiscible interfaces }

At an interface separating two materials, $m_{source}$ and 
$m_{dest}$, with possible phase 
change\cite{palmore2019volume,villegas2016ghost,VAHAB2021,
vahab2016adaptive,ye2024improved}, 
the interface
velocity is either:
\begin{eqnarray}
\vec{u}_{\Gamma}=\vec{u}_{m_{source}}+
  \frac{|\dot{m}|}{\rho_{m_{source}}}\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
\end{eqnarray}
or
\begin{eqnarray}
\vec{u}_{\Gamma}=\vec{u}_{m_{dest}}+
  \frac{|\dot{m}|}{\rho_{m_{dest}}}\vec{n}_{\Gamma}.
\end{eqnarray}
$\vec{n}_{\Gamma}$ is the interface normal vector pointing from 
material $m_{dest}$ to material $m_{source}$.
\par\noindent
The rate of mass transfer is,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\dot{m}=\frac{
		k_{m_{source}}\nabla T_{m_{source}} -
		k_{m_{dest}}\nabla T_{m_{dest}}
              }{L}\cdot \vec{n}_{\Gamma}.
	\label{heat_flux}
\end{eqnarray}
The phase change interface temperature condition is,
\begin{eqnarray}
	T_{m_{source}}=T_{m_{dest}}=
	T_{\mbox{saturation}}(P_{\mbox{equilibrium}})
\end{eqnarray}

\begin{comment}
For evaporation, the following rate of mass transfer should match that in
(\ref{heat_flux}):
\begin{eqnarray}
	\dot{m}=\frac{-\vec{n}_{m_{s}}\cdot\nabla Y_{m_{d}}\rho_{m_{d}}D}
	{Y^{\Gamma}-1}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{comment}

Let the tensor $\sigma$ represent pressure, viscosity, viscoelastic, and
elastic contributions to the momentum equations.  One has:
\begin{eqnarray}
	\sigma=-p\EyeTen+\tau.
\end{eqnarray}
The stress jump conditions are
\begin{eqnarray}
\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\cdot (-\sigma_{m_{sourse}}+\sigma_{m_{dest}}) 
\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}= -\gamma\kappa_{\Gamma}-
\dot{m}^{2}(
\frac{1}{\rho_{m_{source}}}-
\frac{1}{\rho_{m_{dest}}})
\end{eqnarray}
\begin{eqnarray}
	(\vec{t}_{i,tangent})_{m_{sourse}}\cdot
	(-\sigma_{m_{source}}+\sigma_{m_{dest}})
	\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}=0 \hspace{10pt} i=1,2
\end{eqnarray}
The interface curvature is,
\begin{eqnarray}
	\kappa_{\Gamma}=\nabla\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
\end{eqnarray}

\subsection{Time step stability restrictions}

The time step, $\Delta t$, must satisfy the following 
conditions\cite{ye2024improved}.  
\begin{description}
\item[1. CFL condition] In order that characteristics do not 
 collide, the following CFL condition holds:
 \begin{eqnarray*}
 \Delta t\le 
  \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mbox{min}\Delta x}{\mbox{max}|\vec{u}|_{\infty}} 
 \end{eqnarray*}
\item[2. Phase change CFL condition]
 \begin{eqnarray*}
 \Delta t\le 
  \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mbox{min}\Delta x}{\mbox{max}
   |\vec{u}^{\mbox{phase change}}|_{\infty}} 
 \end{eqnarray*}
\item[3. Surface tension stability constraint] 
 \begin{eqnarray*}
 \Delta t\le\frac{1}{2}(\mbox{min} \Delta x^{3/2}) 
   \sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\Gamma}}{2\pi\sigma_{\Gamma}}}
 \end{eqnarray*}
\item[4. Elastic material stability constraint]
 \begin{eqnarray*}
 U_{elastic}=\sqrt{\frac{8 s : s}{\rho G}}
 \end{eqnarray*}
 \begin{eqnarray*}
 \Delta t\le 
  \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mbox{min}\Delta x}{\mbox{max}|U_{elastic}|}
 \end{eqnarray*}
\end{description}
We remark that, in contrast to \cite{ye2024improved}, we choose the
EI-LE advection 
algorithm\cite{jemison2014compressible,scardovelli2003interface} rather 
than the Weymouth and Yue algorithm\cite{weymouth2010conservative},
since the EI-LE has a less stringent time step constraint.  The
drawback of using EI-LE is that it is not volume preserving in 3D; but this
is inconsequential for the present paper since we have deloped
an algorithm for exactly conserving the volume.  Please see
Algorithm \ref{mass_correction} and Figure \ref{mass_cor_fig}.

 \State $\phi^{n+1,(k+1)}_{m}\gets \phi^{n}_{m}+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\vec{u}^{extend}\cdot\nabla \phi_{m} dt \hspace{10pt} m\in[1,M_{e}]$ 
 \State $\phi_{m}^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets\mbox{Extend}(\phi_{m}^{n+1,(k+1)})$ \Comment{from fluid region into elastic}

 \State $\dot{m}_{correct}\gets\mbox{Mass Correction}(\mbox{Mass Current},\mbox{Mass Target})$
 \For{$m=1,\ldots,M_{e}$}
 \State $\vec{u}^{extend}\gets \mbox{Extend}(\vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)})$ \Comment{from elastic region $m$ to outside}

 \State $\left( \begin{array}{c} \vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)} \\

  \left( \begin{array}{c} 
    \vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t\frac{\nabla p^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\tilde{\rho}} \\
  \tilde{p}-\Delta t \tilde{\rho} c^{2} \nabla\cdot\vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)}\end{array}\right)$
\State $E^{n+1,(k+1)}\gets E^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}-
	\Delta t\frac{\nabla\cdot(\vec{u}p)^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\tilde{\rho}}$
 \State $\vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets\vec{u}^{\ast,(k+1)}+\Delta t \frac{\nabla\cdot (2(\mu+\mu_{\mbox{Lax}})D^{\ast\ast,(k+1)})}{\tilde{\rho}}$ \Comment{$\vec{u}_{m_{e}}$ clamped}
 \State $\vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}\gets\vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t\frac{\sum_{\Gamma}\gamma_{\Gamma}\kappa_{\Gamma}\nabla H_{\Gamma}}{\tilde{\rho}}$
 \State $\left( \begin{array}{c} \vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)} \\ 
           p^{n+1,(k+1)} \end{array}\right)\gets
  \left( \begin{array}{c} 
    \vec{u}^{\ast\ast,(k+1)}-\Delta t\frac{\nabla p^{n+1,(k+1)}}{\tilde{\rho}} \\
  \tilde{p}-\Delta t \tilde{\rho} c^{2}\nabla\cdot\vec{u}^{n+1,(k+1)}\end{array}\right)$ \Comment{$\vec{u}_{m_{e}}$ clamped}
 \State $k \gets k+1$
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Phase change algorithm\label{phasechange_alg}}
\begin{algorithmic}[1] % [1] enables line numbering
\State For each cell in a narrow band about the interface, use the
  closest point map to find the corresponding closest point on the
  interface.  Approximate the phase change speed, $|\dot{m}|/\rho$ 
  at the closest point. (Figure \ref{mdotproblem})
\State extend the phase change speed $|\dot{m}|/\rho$ 
  further into bulk regions.
\State Advect the volume fractions using unsplit CISL advection
  with velocity $|\dot{m}|\vec{n}_{\Gamma}/\rho$.
\State The overall mass correction in order to conserve mass due to
  expansion or compression is:
  $M_{cor}=\sum (F_{dest}^{new}-F_{dest}^{old})V_{grid}(\rho_{m_{s}}-\rho_{m_{d}})$.
\State If the interface velocity is in the form 
  $\vec{u}_{I}=\vec{u}_{m_{d}}+\frac{|\dot{m}|}{\rho_{m_{d}}}\vec{n}_{\Gamma}$,
  then distribute $M_{cor}$ at least two cells deep into 
  the source material $m_{s}$.
\State If the interface velocity is in the form 
  $\vec{u}_{I}=\vec{u}_{m_{s}}+\frac{|\dot{m}|}{\rho_{m_{s}}}\vec{n}_{\Gamma}$,
  then distribute $M_{cor}$ at least two cells deep into
  the destination material $m_{d}$.
\State If the material with the distributed mass, $M_{cor}$, is an
  incompressible material, then $M_{cor}$ becomes a source term for the
  velocity projection equation, otherwise, the density is incremented 
  accordingly.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
